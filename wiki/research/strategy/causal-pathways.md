# Causal paths from AI competition to conflict

This table is the corpus author's synthesis.
The sources support mechanisms. They do not prove the full paths.

| Path | Necessary links | Evidence to watch | A result that weakens the path |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feared AI breakthrough | Leaders perceive a large lead. They expect lasting military effects. They believe coercion can help. | Official threat assessments, emergency powers, public demands. | The lead is temporary, narrow, or hard to deploy. |
| Rush to deploy | Competitive pressure shortens testing. A system receives broad access. Its failure matters in a crisis. | Test waivers, changed mandates, incident reports. | Strong review and limited permissions contain the failure. |
| False warning | AI output enters a trusted channel. Humans accept it. A response changes the rival's behavior. | Conflicting sensors, revised assessments, audit records. | Independent checks reject the output before action. |
| Wrong attribution | An incident is linked to a country. Leaders infer state intent without enough evidence. | Public blame, incomplete forensic reports, later corrections. | Joint evidence shows a different cause or operator. |
| Chip denial | Political or legal action changes supply. Substitutes are inadequate. Leaders connect the loss to national security. | Actual licenses, orders, deliveries, and capacity. | Firms adapt, restrictions narrow, or negotiations preserve supply. |
| Maritime crisis | Coercion affects shipping. Affected states respond. An incident produces casualties or loss of control. | Verified restrictions, traffic changes, consultations. | Access is restored and both sides can claim a limited result. |
| Nuclear entanglement | An event affects a shared function. Leaders fear damage to deterrence. Time for review shrinks. | Official alerts, communication outages, changed posture. | Clear scope and redundant systems preserve confidence. |
| Alliance disagreement | Allies face different costs. Their responses diverge. One side misreads those responses. | Joint statements, domestic debate, policy exceptions. | Consultation clarifies limited commitments. |
| Commercial conflict | A firm fears losing access or IP. It seeks state protection. The dispute becomes a security issue. | Regulatory cases, public complaints, evidence of theft. | Commercial remedies resolve the dispute. |
| Shared emergency | Both sides face harmful AI behavior. They recognize common exposure. They coordinate containment. | Shared incident formats and verifiable corrective action. | Secrecy or blame blocks practical cooperation. |

The first, second, and last paths draw on [RAND's incentives analysis](https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PEA4189-1.html).
The preventive path also draws on [Breakwater](https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PEA4703-1.html).
The warning path draws on [CSET's decision-risk analysis](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/reducing-the-risks-of-artificial-intelligence-for-military-decision-advantage/).
The entanglement path draws on [Acton's paper abstract](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/posts/2018/12/escalation-through-entanglement-how-the-vulnerability-of-command-and-control-systems-raises-the-risks-of-an-inadvertent-nuclear-war).
The chip and maritime paths use the [Taiwan research](../taiwan-chips/README.md).
The incident paths use the [AI research](../ai-ecosystem/README.md).

## Test AI's role

For each scenario, ask what changes if the AI factor is removed.
The game can then classify AI as:

- A motive for conflict.
- A means used in conflict.
- An amplifier of an existing dispute.
- A source of error or uncertainty.
- A means to improve defense or cooperation.

More than one label can apply.
The labels are analytical. They do not measure causal effect size.
