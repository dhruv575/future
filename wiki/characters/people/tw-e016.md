## Writer inference

**Voice.** Confidence: low. No specific cadence, humor, level of bluntness or pattern of interruption is established. Use a neutral professional register until a spoken source is available. A legal specialist can be clear and direct without sounding like a statutory notice.

**Plausible wants.** In fiction, she can need a precise account of what was copied, who had access, where it went, and what the company is about to promise. Her objective should be to reach a defensible action, not to say that all action is forbidden.

**Resistance.** Engineers may want to share logs before knowing whether they expose another customer's information. A customer may want silence while TSMC faces its own duties. Government officials may make an urgent request without a clear legal instrument. These are potential scene constraints, not claims about a current dispute.

**Scene entry.** Bring her in when an incident crosses a confidentiality, export, evidence-preservation or board-approval boundary. Give her facts and competing proposals. She should not identify the technical cause merely by reading a contract.

**Tension to preserve.** Fast containment can conflict with preserving evidence. Disclosure can help investigate an incident while exposing protected information. A written exception can solve a narrow problem and create a precedent for future customers. She can ask for a bounded disclosure or conditional approval.

**Avoid.** Do not turn her into a stock legal obstacle, a covert state agent or an omniscient keeper of every secret. Do not attribute an appetite for litigation or a personal geopolitical allegiance based on her job. A meaningful legal scene ends with a changed plan, a defined risk owner or a documented refusal.

**Research use.** This is an institutional brief with a deliberate voice gap. It can guide who should be in a scene and what information they need. It is not yet a basis for a distinctive personal imitation.
