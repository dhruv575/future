# Use the research in a scene

Start with the person's problem.
Then decide what they can do, what they know, and what they need from someone else.
Write dialogue after those decisions.

## Keep three layers separate

| Layer | Contents | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public evidence | A dated role, statement, agreement, or research result | Attach a source and its limits. |
| Interpretation | A reading of public voice, interests, or tensions | Label it as writer inference. |
| Scenario state | A future office, private goal, promise, belief, or event | Define it as fiction and keep it consistent. |

A research paper can support a mechanism or a conditional prediction.
It cannot reveal a real person's private motive.
A company position is not automatically the personal view of every employee.

## Build a character's state

Use the [scene template](SCENE-TEMPLATE.md).
Set these items before a decision:

- **Role:** office, date, delegated powers, and required approvals.
- **Knowledge:** facts received, suspected causes, and facts still hidden.
- **Objective:** one result the person needs from this scene.
- **Commitment:** a promise or decision that now has a cost.
- **Constraint:** something the person cannot change alone.
- **Relationship:** what each counterpart can provide or withhold.
- **Threshold:** what new evidence or concession could change the response.

An objective is a writing choice, even when a public statement supports it.
State that choice. Do not present it as recovered private intent.

## Give each meeting a reason

Explain who arranged it, why these people attend, and why the issue reached their level.
A researcher may advise. An executive may negotiate. An official may authorize.
Those functions are distinct.

Use staff for routine briefings and specialist detail.
A known person should enter when their own decision, commitment, or authority matters.
A prior meeting does not establish friendship or an unrestricted private channel.

## Make the disagreement concrete

A useful scene needs an unresolved request.
Examples include a test result, an allocation, a deadline, a public statement, or permission to act.
Give the counterpart a reason to resist and a possible concession.

## Write a voice, not a catchphrase

Use the person's way of making an argument.
Notice how they answer a challenge, qualify a claim, choose an example, or refuse a request.
Match the source setting to the scene.

Use simple language in narration and interface text.
Keep natural contractions and sentence variation in dialogue.
Do not force every person into the same formal briefing style.

A translation can support the meaning of an argument.
It is weaker evidence for English rhythm.
Do not write an accent from transcript errors.
A paper supports an analytical position better than a private speaking style.

Public optimism can coexist with a hard concession.
A concern about risk can coexist with commercial ambition.
Let the scene reveal the tradeoff through the request and response.

## Make choices leave a record

Each choice must alter something that later scenes use.
Record commitments, permissions, disclosures, delays, resource obligations, and relationships.
Also record which characters learn about the action.

Separate what happens from what a character believes happened.
A reaction requires a path by which information reached that person.
An attribution claim requires evidence; a character's suspicion alone does not make it true.

Scores can summarize effects.
The story still needs a cause for each effect.
A result should follow the selected action, the current state, and other actors' responses.
Use uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a fixed result.

## Review the draft

Read each character's lines without the name tags.
Check whether their purpose and argument differ.
Then check authority, knowledge, chronology, and source support.

Remove biography delivered to people who already know it.
Remove repeated explanations that do not change a decision.
Keep a source list outside the conversation box.
Label invented dialogue as fiction at the game level.

Before release, refresh time-sensitive facts.
For a low-confidence voice, get a longer direct sample or keep the speaking role limited.
